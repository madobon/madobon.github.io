Based on the visual evidence, this is a photograph of a famous **Aztec (Mexica) stone sculpture**, likely housed in the **Museo del Templo Mayor** in Mexico City.

Here is a detailed breakdown of what is shown:

**1. Subject and Identity**
*   **Deity:** The figure is widely identified as a representation of **Mictlantecuhtli**, the Aztec god of the dead and ruler of the underworld (Mictlan).
*   **Key Feature:** The most striking feature is the **skull-like face**. It features large, hollow eye sockets, a triangular nasal cavity, and a grimacing mouth with visible teeth, which are classic iconographic elements of death in Mesoamerican art.

**2. Physical Description**
*   **Posture:** The figure is depicted in a seated position with an upright posture. The legs are bent, and the hands (formed into fists) rest heavily on the knees. This is a pose of authority and permanence.
*   **Headdress:** The figure wears an elaborate, tall headdress. While weathered, it appears to have vertical ornaments, possibly representing feathers or paper strips, which were symbols of high status or priesthood.
*   **Attire:** On the chest, there is a large, circular pectoral ornament. The lower body appears to be covered in a loincloth or skirt, typical of Aztec noble or divine attire.
*   **Material:** It is carved from a single block of dark grey volcanic stone (likely andesite or basalt), which was the standard material for monumental Aztec sculpture.

**3. Context and Location**
*   **Setting:** The clean white walls and museum lighting indicate this is on display in a gallery.
*   **Origin:** Statues of this style and quality were typically found in the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan (modern-day Mexico City), specifically near the Templo Mayor or in associated buildings like the "House of the Eagles" (Casa de las Águilas).

**4. Cultural Significance**
*   In Aztec cosmology, death was not an end but a transition. Mictlantecuhtli was a powerful deity who required respect and offerings. Sculptures like this were often placed in temples or burial contexts to guard the sacred spaces or honor the dead.
*   The combination of a human body with a skull head emphasizes the duality of life and death, a central theme in Aztec religion.
